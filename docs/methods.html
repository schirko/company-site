How We Test the Apps

A model that looks good on the data it learned from proves very little. Here is how each app is checked before we trust it, the actual results, and where each one still falls short.

The rules every app follows

  • Test on data the model hasn't seen. Prices are tested forward in time: fit on past sales, predict the next one, compare with what really happened. Yields are tested on whole regions and whole years the model never saw.
  • Beat something simple. Every model is compared with the rule of thumb it replaces, such as last week's price or the average yield. If it can't beat that, it doesn't ship.
  • Ranges come from real misses. An "80% range" means that in testing, 8 in 10 real answers landed inside it.
  • Say what fails. Each app lists the cases it gets wrong, and why.

Honest Ranges

Every answer comes with a range, and the range is checked against reality instead of just claimed.

Here's an example from Herd Planner's price forecast (September 2026): "In December, 550 lb steers at Oklahoma City: about $405/cwt, likely between $365 and $470." That's an 80% range. It should hold the real price about 8 times in 10.

How a Range Is Made

We replay the last few years. At each past month, the app forecasts using only what was known then, and we write down how far off it was. The range comes from those real misses, not from a textbook formula that assumes prices behave nicely.

How It's Checked

We count how often the real price actually landed inside. If an "80%" range only caught 6 in 10, it was overconfident, and we widen it and say so. The app does this on its own: after a miss, the next range gets a little wider; after a hit, a little narrower. When cattle prices fell sharply in 2026, the ranges widened instead of pretending to know.

Herd Planner

The question: what is each animal worth at today's sale barn, and should I keep or sell it?

Calf prices, tested on 26 sales it never saw

For each of 26 weekly auctions, the price model learned only from the sales before it, then priced every lot. Its "typical miss" (the median error) is compared with the usual shortcut: look up what similar cattle brought at the last sale.

MethodTypical missAnimals it can price
Last-sale lookup2.85%82%
Herd Planner's price model2.52%100%

In the same test, 80% of real prices landed between 4.1% below and 4.7% above the model's estimate: about ±$100 on a $2,200 calf. That is the range the app shows.

Your own sale barn

Most barns report too rarely to model on their own, so the app starts from Oklahoma City's prices and adds each barn's usual premium or discount. On five barns, that cut the typical miss from 6.5–7.5% (Oklahoma City prices alone) to 2.7–4.3%. Each barn's range comes from its own backtest.

Catching typos in weights

Graded on 33,000 simulated weights with known mistakes planted in them, the weight check caught 77% of big typos, and 97% of the weights it flagged really were wrong: about one false alarm per 2,000 good weights. The textbook method (mean and standard deviation) caught only 24%, because one big typo hides the others.

Keep or sell

The heifer keep-or-sell math reproduces a published University of Arkansas worked example. The herd growth planner runs 1,000 possible futures for each keep-rate, with prices moving by the 15% a year measured in five years of USDA auction data.

Where it falls short

  • The biggest source of error is the whole market moving between one sale and the next, which no model of past sales can see coming.
  • In a falling market it prices a little high more often than a little low.

Yield Predictor

The question: what will a county's corn yield be, given its weather, soils and irrigation?

Scores below are R²: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than guessing the average yield, and below 0 is worse than guessing.

How you test changes the answer

The same model, scored three ways on Nebraska counties from 2000 to 2025:

TestGradient boostingDetrended boosting
Random counties held out0.570.54
Whole districts held out0.400.31
Trained to 2018, tested on 2019 on−0.42−0.05

Holding out random counties flatters the model, because neighboring counties in the same year are near copies of each other. And the model that won the easy tests failed worst on future years: tree models can't predict a yield higher than any they've seen, and yields keep rising. Fitting the long-run trend first, then modelling the rest, brought the future-years score back to about zero.

Those are weather-only scores. Adding the share of acres irrigated (worked out from USDA acreage figures, since it isn't published) lifted the district score by 0.27, more than any amount of model tuning. With irrigation, soils, elevation, crop rotation and spring weather, the future-years score reached about 0.5.

Does it work in a state it has never seen?

Trained on Nebraska only, then applied cold to Iowa's counties:

Predicting Iowa with…R²
Nebraska's average yield (the floor)−0.26
The Nebraska-trained model0.51
A model trained on Iowa itself (the ceiling)0.70

It got 80% of the way from floor to ceiling. On counties it hasn't seen, a typical miss is about 14 bushels an acre in Nebraska and 10 in Iowa.

Where it falls short

  • The misses form a map, not random noise: it overpredicts the sandy, high west of Nebraska and underpredicts the northeast, in every year.
  • It has no wind data, so the August 2020 derecho is its clearest single failure.
  • In Iowa, the link between spring weather and yield held until 2018 and then stopped holding. The app keeps those features and reports the change rather than hiding it.
  • It explains past seasons. It has never been tested for predicting a season while it's still growing.

Grazing Planner

The question: how much grass will a pasture grow this season, and how many head can it carry?

Every Nebraska county's season from 2011 to 2025 was forecast using only the seasons before it, the way the app would have seen it at the time. Skill compares with always assuming a normal year: 0 is no better, 1 is perfect.

Before June nothing beats "a normal year", so the app says it's too early to tell. The 80% ranges held 77 to 87% of the time from June 1 on.

Against Grass-Cast

Grass-Cast is the public grassland forecast from USDA and partners. Rerun on the same dates for the 40 counties it covers, 2017 to 2025, ours scored higher from mid-June on: July 1, 0.69 against 0.36; August 1, 0.86 against 0.31. On June 1 the edge was small and not certain. One caution: both were graded against the satellite estimate our model learns from, which favors ours.

Does "enough grass 9 years in 10" hold?

Every county and season 2011 to 2025, stocked the way the app advises and checked against the grass that really grew. Stocking for a normal year ran short 48% of the time. The app's 9-in-10 head count ran short 10% of the time in June and 9% by July 1, for about 12% fewer head than a normal year. Turning out 90% of a normal year and checking again on July 1 (sell, or add when there's room) ran short 9% of the time and grazed 91% of a normal year.

Where it falls short

  • It forecasts a county average from satellite estimates, not your pasture. Walk your pasture.
  • In the big years it gets the direction right but the size too small: 2022 ended 20% below normal statewide; the June 15 forecast said 11% below.
  • A bad season hits most of the state at once: even stocking for 9 years in 10, a third or more of Nebraska ran short together in 1 or 2 of the 15 seasons tested.
  • The satellite data runs about two weeks behind, so a July 1 check knows the grass through about mid-June.

Farm Equipment Planner

The question: does a tractor, combine or implement pay for itself on my farm?

Most of this app is established farm economics, so its first test is matching the published sources:

  • Machine size: the acres-per-hour formula matches all 64 rows of Iowa State University's field capacity table.
  • Cost to own: follows the ASABE and Iowa State machinery cost method.
  • Harvest delays: each county's real falls from 2000 on are replayed day by day, using the same workable-day rule as the Yield Predictor (checked: 0 of 104 county-years differ).
  • Resale value: tested first on practice sales with known answers planted in them. It recovered them (11.5% a year lost to age, against 12% planted), and its 80% range held about 80% of new sales. It stays closed until auction companies and dealers have shared at least 50 real sales from two or more companies.

Where it falls short

  • The workable-day rule only sees rain, not snow or frozen ground, so late-fall answers are the least reliable.
  • About 25 years of weather can't show a one-in-50-year disaster.
  • County yields are averages; a single farm's yields swing more than its county's.

Where the data comes from

Auction prices from USDA Market News; county yields and acreage from USDA NASS; weather from NASA POWER; grassland forage from the Rangeland Analysis Platform (USDA Agricultural Research Service and partners); soils from USDA's Soil Data Access; elevation from USGS. All of it is public. None of these agencies endorses the apps, and any error in the analysis is ours.

The apps are decision aids, not financial, agronomic or insurance advice. Each app's own terms say more.
